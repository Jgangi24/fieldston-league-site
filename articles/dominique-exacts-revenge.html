Dominique Exacts Revenge, Shatters the Competition in Epic Dunk Contest

Thirty-nine years after Chicago robbed him in front of his own hometown judges, Wilkins finally got the ending he always thought he deserved — and brought his own glass to make sure of it.

By Larry Kuppleton

Fieldston Core Four Fantasy League · August 8, 2026

FLAMINGTON — Everybody who was alive for it still has an opinion about 1988: Chicago Stadium, hometown judges, and a scorecard that gave Michael Jordan the trophy over Dominique Wilkins by a margin most people who watched it still don't believe. Wilkins has spent parts of four decades being polite about it in interviews. He was not polite about it Saturday night.

“I don't need anybody to agree with me anymore,” Wilkins said, sweat still coming off him an hour after the final horn. “I just needed the ball back in my hands one more time.”

He got it. And when the night ended, there was glass all over the court to prove it.

The Field

Eight dunkers took the floor for what the panel is already calling the deepest field in league history: Michael Jordan and Dominique Wilkins, running it back nearly forty years later; Vince Carter and Julius Erving, generational bookends of the same aerial lineage; David Thompson and Tracy McGrady, both threatening to redefine “effortless”; Dwight Howard, the only pure power dunker in the field; and, improbably, Mac McClung — a fringe roster piece by way of two-way contracts and G-League highlight reels, and the only man on the floor with more actual dunk contest trophies than anyone else in the building.

Judging the field alongside Leslie, Newton, and Chung was a late addition to the panel: Mr. Whitman, longtime English teacher and, by several accounts in the building Saturday, the toughest grader “Mike” ever had — dunk contest included. Whitman scored with a red pen energy no other judge on the panel could match, and made no secret that decades of grading essays had left him allergic to showmanship for its own sake.

Before the first dunk went up, someone asked Jordan if he was relieved LeBron James hadn't entered. Jordan didn't blink. He just gave the room a long, cold look — the one that's ended more postgame questions than any answer ever has.

“You can't expect to win a dunk contest,” he said, “if you ain't ever entered a dunk contest.”

Nobody followed up.

The first round went the way most first rounds go with this much talent in one gym — Erving turned a baseline reverse into a poem, Carter did something with a bounce pass off the backboard that made all five judges stand up, and McGrady quietly put up a 49 that nobody saw coming. But it was McClung who stole the round. A journeyman by the league's own admission, dunking between Hall of Famers, and somehow still in the conversation.

“That's the beauty of this event,” said judge Lisa Leslie. “The résumé doesn't dunk. The man dunks.”

Judge Isaac Newton, working his first Slam Dunk Contest, was overheard remarking that Carter's baseline attempt had “briefly suspended several of my own laws.”

Judge Flavor Flav, for his part, did not use words so much as an airhorn, several times, at volumes that may have violated arena code.

Whitman, for his part, saved his sharpest grading for Jordan — a habit, apparently, that goes back further than this contest. “I've been giving Mike a hard time since he was seventeen,” Whitman said dryly, before docking a Jordan first-round dunk half a point for what he called “showing his work but not the thesis.” Jordan, to the room's visible delight, just shook his head and laughed like a man who'd heard that particular tone before.

McClung's run ended in the semifinals — a walk-off flush from McGrady that the judges gave a 50, and that Newton reportedly called “the cleanest parabola I have seen from a human being.” McClung, gracious in defeat, pulled Wilkins aside on his way off the floor.

“Man told me he wished Spud could've been here to see it,” McClung said. “I told him I wished I could've seen Spud do it live. We were both a little sad about that, honestly.”

It was, everyone would later agree, the last quiet moment of the night.

The Final

Jordan and Wilkins met in the final exactly the way the league office had hoped they might when they built the bracket, and exactly the way nobody in Chicago in 1988 saw coming: even. Jordan opened with a free-throw-line take-off that drew a near-perfect card. Wilkins answered with a two-ball reverse windmill that had judge Connie Chung — normally reserved for hard news — visibly rattled on the broadcast.

“I've covered wars,” Chung said afterward. “That dunk had me more nervous.”

Jordan's response was vintage: a between-the-legs, one-handed finish that split the difference between showmanship and disrespect. The crowd gave it the ovation. The judges gave it a 49. It was, for a moment, starting to look like Chicago all over again.

Then the lights dimmed, and two men in warmups nobody in the building had introduced walked out to center court, wheeling something with them.

Darryl Dawkins and Jerome Lane — the two men most responsible for the existence of shatterproof backboards in the modern game — pushed a full late-1970s rig onto the floor, glass backboard and all, and stepped back.

Wilkins didn't say a word. He took the ball, walked to the top of the key, and went.

The windmill was clean. The landing wasn't. Glass came down across the free-throw line in sheets, the rim hung sideways off a shattered board, and Wilkins stood underneath it with both arms out before the crowd noise had even finished building. Nobody bothered to hold up scorecards. There was, at that point, no backboard left to hold a score against.

“You can't judge that,” Newton reportedly told his fellow panelists. “There is no denominator large enough.”

Jordan, to his credit, was the first one to reach Wilkins under the wreckage, and the two shared a long handshake at center court while stadium staff started sweeping.

“Thirty-nine years,” Wilkins said. “I think we're square now.”

Jordan didn't disagree.

Chicago never got to see Dominique Wilkins get the ending everyone thought he deserved. Flamington did — and it came with glass on the floor to make sure nobody could take it back this time.
