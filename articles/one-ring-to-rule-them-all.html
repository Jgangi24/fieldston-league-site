One Ring to Rule Them All: Can Elgin Baylor Finally Get His?

He walked away nine games into the season that ended in a championship without him. Fifty-some years later, Laurel Canyon might be giving the league's most unsung legend one more shot at the thing that always got away.

By Lois Kranberries

Fieldston Core Four Fantasy League · August 11, 2026

FLAMINGTON — The cruelest sentence in basketball history might be this one: Elgin Baylor retired on October 31, 1971, nine games into a season the Lakers would finish by winning 33 in a row and the championship that followed it. He never got a ring. Not because he wasn't good enough — nobody who ever guarded him would tell you that — but because of timing so bad it reads like something out of a Greek tragedy. The best player never to win it all walked away right before the door opened.

He's still walking through doors. He's just a lot better at finding them lately.

Baylor is averaging 24.5 points, 6.7 rebounds and 16.9 GmSc a night for a Laurel Canyon team that's climbed to fourth in the standings and hasn't looked back. It hasn't always been smooth — a 6-point, 8-turnover night in a February loss to Flamington sits in the game log a few rows above a 44-point eruption against Blankendorf that also happened to be a win. But the shape of it, across 59 games, is unmistakable: this is still one of the most dangerous players in the league, and for the first time in half a century, he's on a team that might actually let him find out what a championship feels like.

“He Never Said a Word About It”

Nobody has carried the weight of Baylor's retirement longer than Jerry West, his running mate for over a decade and the man who watched the 1972 title get won from close enough to feel responsible for it.

“I've thought about it my whole life,” West said. “We used to talk about getting one together, and then he was gone, and we got it without him. He never once made me feel bad about it. Never brought it up. That's just who he was — but I felt it anyway.”

Asked what it would mean to see Baylor finally get there — even in a season neither of them expected to be sharing a locker room again — West didn't hesitate.

“It'd mean more to me than anything I ever won myself,” he said. “I owe him that.”

A High-Flyer Before the Word Existed

It's easy to forget, watching the modern game's highlight reels, that the vocabulary for what Baylor does — hang time, footwork in the air, angles nobody else could see — didn't exist until he invented the need for it. Players who came a generation after him still talk about him like a rumor they got to watch come true.

“People think the high-flying game started with the guys who dunked on people,” Julius Erving said. “It didn't. It started with a guy who made you believe a man could stop in midair and change his mind. I saw him do something in a shootaround once that I still think about.”

Teammates in Laurel Canyon say the practice-court stories aren't exaggerated. “He does this thing off one foot where the whole gym just stops,” one Laurel Canyon staffer said. “Guys who've been in this league twenty years still turn around to watch.”

“My Precious”

There's a nickname that's followed Baylor around the league this season, and it didn't come from a highlight reel. It came from a paperback.

Baylor has been open about his love of Tolkien for years — teammates say a battered copy of The Hobbit has traveled with him on every road trip this season, and more than one has caught him rereading the same dog-eared passage of The Return of the King on team flights. It didn't take long for the league to notice, or for somebody to make the obvious joke stick. Around Laurel Canyon, and increasingly around the rest of the league, he's just as likely to be called Elvin Baylor these days.

He doesn't mind it.

“There's something in those books about carrying a thing a long way, past the point where anybody would blame you for putting it down,” Baylor said. “I've had a long time to think about that.”

The dedication runs deeper than road-trip reading, too. Teammates say he's a fixture at a Renaissance faire near Laurel Canyon's practice facility most summers — cloak, staff, and all — and that he's been known to disappear for an entire off day and come back with stories about jousting matches nobody asked him to attend but everybody wanted to hear about anyway.

More than once this season, he's shown up to shootaround still half in costume — blond wig slightly askew, a quiver of arrows leaned up against his locker like it belonged there. Nobody asks anymore. It's just Tuesday.

“First time I saw him walk in dressed as Legolas, I thought the dude had lost it,” said Charles Barkley, who's shared a locker room with Baylor all season. “I mean I thought he was straight-up weird. Then I actually sat down and listened to him talk about it, and I got sucked all the way in. Now I'm asking him questions like I'm doing homework. Sometimes I'll pretend I'm one of the hobbits, or a dwarf, just to keep him talking — man's got stories for days. Elvin Baylor. We are so lucky to have that man on our team. He's got us out here ready to go kill orcs on the court every single night.”

Nobody in the locker room needed any of it spelled out any further. A man who's spent fifty years chasing a ring that got away from him, still walking, still not putting it down — the joke writes itself, and everybody who makes it means it as a compliment.

“My precious,” Barkley said, grinning, when asked what the trophy would mean to Baylor this year. Baylor, within earshot, didn't argue with the comparison. He just smiled and let it stand.

Halftime, According to Baylor

The speeches are the part nobody warns the newer guys about.

Baylor isn't the head coach, and he isn't the captain, but when Laurel Canyon needs a run in a hurry down the stretch of a close third quarter, he's the one who ends up in the middle of the huddle — and lately, what comes out of him doesn't sound like a normal timeout.

“He'll start talking about small hands doing what great hands couldn't,” said Shaquille O'Neal, another teammate. “About how the ones who carry the heaviest thing the farthest are never the ones anybody expected to. You look around the huddle and half the team's just staring at him like — what game are we about to play?”

Barkley put it more simply a beat later: “He talks about a thing having power over you if you let it, and how the whole trick is wanting it bad enough to go get it without it eating you alive on the way there. I don't know if he's talking about a basketball or a piece of jewelry half the time. But we win the third quarter a lot.”

Baylor, asked directly whether he's aware his pep talks sound like they were pulled from a paperback, allowed himself a rare grin.

“Every good story's about somebody who wanted something bad enough to walk a long way for it,” he said. “I don't see why that shouldn't work at halftime too.”

A Chance, Not a Guarantee

Laurel Canyon's not the best team in the league — Queens and Jackson Heights are still ahead of them, and the margin for error from here on is thin. But for a player who has spent fifty years as the answer to a trivia question about heartbreak, “a real chance” might be the only thing that was ever missing.

Baylor, for his part, isn't interested in the framing.

“I don't play for what I didn't get in 1972,” he said. “I play because I still love it. If it happens, it happens because we earned it — not because somebody owes me anything.”

West, standing nearby, didn't look like he entirely believed him. Nobody in the room did either.
