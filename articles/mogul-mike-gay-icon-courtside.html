Mogul Mike: The Gay Icon Courtside

He is passionate, he is authentic, he is as gay as the day is long. And his fans wouldn't have him any other way.

By Larry Kuppleton

Fieldston Core Four Fantasy League · August 7, 2026

Every basketball league has its stars. Every league has its villains. Every league has the owner who somehow becomes more famous than the people actually playing.

And then there's Mogul Mike.

To his admirers, Mike Wiltworthy Kobronabitch isn't merely a wealthy owner with an appetite for the spotlight. He's a gay icon. A walking celebration of self-expression. A man who has apparently decided that if you're going to own two basketball franchises, you might as well do it while being spectacularly, unapologetically yourself.

And his fans absolutely love him for it.

“He is passionate, he is authentic, he is as gay as the day is long!” said one longtime supporter, summing up the Mogul Mike phenomenon in a sentence that has since appeared on at least three homemade T-shirts and one suspiciously expensive arena banner.

It isn't difficult to understand the appeal.

Mogul Mike has never subscribed to the traditional philosophy that an owner should sit quietly in a luxury suite, nod occasionally at a general manager and otherwise behave as though the basketball court is happening several miles away. Mike doesn't observe the action.

Mike participates in the experience.

Tinted aviators. Velvet. Black. Dramatic gestures. Celebrity friends. Courtside celebrations. Arguments with officials. Unsolicited tactical opinions. The occasional decision to become physically involved in a celebration that was, technically, intended for the players.

This isn't an owner trying to blend in.

This is a man who has turned being himself into a competitive advantage.

And nowhere is that more apparent than in the culture of his teams.

The Los Angeles Magic Wiltworthy Kobronabitches and the Laurel Canyon Naughty Chauffeurs don't simply have rosters. They have personalities. They have flair. They have swagger. They have the unmistakable sense that somebody, somewhere, has decided that basketball should be considerably more fabulous than the rulebook requires.

That somebody is Mogul Mike.

Critics might call it excessive. They might argue that owners shouldn't be so emotionally invested, so visible, or so willing to make themselves part of the show. But that criticism misses the point. Mike's teams reflect the person who owns them, and that authenticity has become part of their identity.

His players don't just play for wins.

They play in an environment where individuality is celebrated, where flamboyance isn't something to apologize for, and where nobody seems particularly interested in pretending that personality belongs outside the lines.

For a certain segment of the fan base, that's enormously important.

Basketball has always had its outsiders, its originals, its people who refuse to fit neatly into whatever box somebody else built for them. Mogul Mike has simply taken that principle and put it courtside, dressed it in velvet, handed it a pair of tinted glasses and given it ownership papers.

The result is something larger than a team culture.

It's a statement.

You can be successful without becoming conventional. You can be wealthy without becoming boring. You can be powerful without pretending you don't care. And, perhaps most importantly, you can be exactly who you are — even when exactly who you are happens to be extraordinarily, gloriously gay.

That is why Mogul Mike has become more than an owner to his admirers.

He's an inspiration.

A flamboyant reminder that authenticity is its own kind of power. A courtside icon for fans who see something familiar in his refusal to tone himself down. A man who has built basketball teams that look, feel and occasionally behave exactly like the person who owns them.

Two franchises. One enormous personality.

And a growing legion of fans who wouldn't change a thing.

Mogul Mike isn't trying to be everyone's cup of tea.

He's too busy being fabulous.
