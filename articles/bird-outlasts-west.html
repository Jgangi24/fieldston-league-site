Bird Outlasts West in 3-Point Duel for the Ages, Opens Up About a Difficult Season With 3-Deep

Neither man missed for the better part of twenty minutes. Only one of them could leave with the trophy — and only one of them left in the courtesy car.

By Larry Kuppleton

Fieldston Core Four Fantasy League · August 8, 2026

FLAMINGTON — Larry Bird has never been shy about wanting things badly, and Saturday night he wanted this one badly enough to say so out loud, more than once, to anyone who would listen. By the time it was over, he'd earned the right to.

The Field

Eight of the league's purest shooters took turns at the line: Stephen Curry and Klay Thompson, San Francisco's backcourt pairing that's kept a lost season watchable; Chris Mullin and Jerry West, representing a Laurel Canyon team built to score from everywhere; Pete Maravich, quietly holding the best raw percentage in the league; Ray Allen, as automatic a form as the league has ever fielded; Steve Nash; and Bird himself.

The early rounds thinned fast — Maravich caught fire on the money ball and still fell one rack short of the final, Mullin and Nash traded 21s that canceled each other out, and Curry's round ended with a rack that clipped iron four times running, uncharacteristic and, by his own account afterward, “just one of those nights.” Klay Thompson made the semifinals before running into the two men who'd clearly decided this competition belonged to them and no one else.

The Final

What happened between Bird and West over the next twenty minutes will be the entry point for this contest in every recap for years. Both men shot the first rack clean. Both men shot the second rack clean. By the fourth consecutive perfect round, the arena had stopped scoring in its head and started just watching.

With no misses to separate them, the two moved the competition to half court — first make wins. West, seventy-some feet from a basket he's been shooting at longer than most of the building has been alive, banked one in on his second attempt. Bird answered on his third. They kept going. Six rounds. Six pairs of makes. The gap between “these two are showing off” and “these two do not know how to lose to each other” collapsed somewhere around round four.

Bird broke it on the seventh half-court round, a swish that didn't touch the rim, while West's attempt caught back iron and spun out.

West didn't wait for the trophy ceremony. He didn't wait for a handshake. He walked straight to the courtesy car parked at half court — the runner-up prize — got in, and drove it out through the tunnel himself, tires audibly protesting on the hardwood the whole way. Arena staff were still looking at the skid marks on the court an hour later.

Bird didn't chase him. Anyone who's watched Jerry West lose at anything could have told you not to.

“The Season's Lost. I'm Not.”

What came next wasn't really a trophy interview. It turned into something closer to a sit-down.

“I wanted this one bad,” Bird said, still holding the trophy like he wasn't ready to put it down. “We're not going anywhere this year with 3-Deep. I know it. Everybody in that locker room knows it. So yeah — this mattered to me more than it probably should have.”

San Francisco sits at 9-40, last in the league, twenty and a half games back with a losing streak that's stretched past a month. Bird didn't dodge any of it.

“It's been a hard year,” he said. “But I've actually really enjoyed this group. Chris Mullin. Draymond. Klay — man, Klay is the strangest guy I've ever shared a locker room with, and I mean that with all the respect in the world. I don't always know what he's talking about. I don't think he always knows what he's talking about. But I like playing with him.”

Asked for an example, Bird didn't hesitate.

“Just this week, Klay comes up to me and goes, ‘Larry, I've been thinking about behind-the-back passes. Does that mean we're playing in reverse? Like, backwards? You know what I mean, Larry?’ And I'm standing there like — Klay, I don't know, man. I told him, go talk to my buddy Robert Parish. Chief. He'll set you straight.”

“So then Klay goes, ‘why do they call him Chief?’ — and before I can even open my mouth, Commissioner Jess Gangi comes flying out of nowhere. ‘Don't answer that, Larry. Please. Don't answer why do they call him Chief.’ So now I still don't know what Klay was actually asking me in the first place.”

Bird was still shaking his head about it when he moved on to the next question.

He grew more pointed when the subject turned to All-Star Saturday itself.

“None of our guys made it,” Bird said. “None of them. And look — I get how the voting works. But Ray Allen should've been on that team. Full stop. What he's doing on a team that's losing the way we're losing, and still shooting the way he shoots — that's a snub, and I'll say it as many times as somebody puts a microphone in front of me.”

Asked about next season, Bird didn't pretend not to understand the question.

“Everybody in this league is basically a free agent when this thing wraps up,” he said. “That's just how it works here. I try not to think about it too much during the season, but yeah — I'd love to play with Kevin McHale again. Tiny Archibald too. Some of my old guys. You don't get many chances to run it back with people, and when the door's open, you think about walking through it.”

He paused, then allowed himself the closest thing to a smile all night.

“But I still love this,” he said. “Competing. Especially with the whole building watching, like tonight. That part never gets old for me. Win or lose the rest of the way — that part, I've still got.”

Jerry West drove himself out of the building before anyone could ask him a single question. Larry Bird stayed and answered every one. By the end of the night, that told you most of what you needed to know about both of them — and about a season that isn't going where either man wanted, for very different reasons.
